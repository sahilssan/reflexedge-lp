The problem

Robots got a big, slow brain — and no brainstem.

Vision-language-action models run at 40–130 W and tens of milliseconds. When a hand enters a workspace or a tool falls, that's an eternity. Today's answer is to slow the robot down or wait for contact. Both are taxes on speed and safety — three faces of one missing layer.

30–100+ ms

Too slow to flinch

Frame pipelines stack exposure, readout, inference, and planning. A 60 mph object travels ~90 cm between frames — the difference between "the robot flinched" and "the incident report."

1.5–5 h

Battery burned on standby

Continuous GPU perception pays wattage even while the robot stands still. Humanoid runtime is now named a top deployment bottleneck. Always-on should cost milliwatts, not watts.

9 ms, after touch

Safety taxes throughput

Proximity zones slow the cell whenever anyone walks past; contact skins react only after impact. Nobody sells fast, spatially-aware, pre-contact reaction — and every recovered second is money.

The solution

A self-contained reflex module. Four functions, one platform.

Event-based sensing feeds embedded processing supervised by a separate safety controller. The value-added perception rides alongside a stop path simple enough to certify.

SAFESTOP

Sub-millisecond hard stop

Detects intrusion into a 3D envelope or collision-course motion and asserts the robot's safe-stop input in under a millisecond — pre-contact protection at speeds contact skins can't allow.

WAKESENSE

Milliwatt always-on wake

Watches the scene at <10 mW while the host compute sleeps, waking the GPU stack only on task-relevant motion — attacking the largest controllable line in the energy budget.

FASTTRACK

Microsecond-resolution tracking

Streams track states for fast objects — catching, insertion verification, conveyor picking at 3–5 m/s — capabilities frame pipelines physically can't deliver through blur and sampling.

SLIPSENSE

Instant grasp-failure detection

A firmly grasped object produces no events relative to the end-effector; slip produces them instantly. Grasp-failure detection orders of magnitude faster than force-torque thresholds.

The architecture

Two lanes. One is smart. One is trusted.

The safety-critical path is deliberately minimal and inspectable. The intelligent path adds value alongside it, never inside it — so the assessor certifies the lane that stops the robot, not the neural network.

Why the split matters: no assessor will put a neural network inside a certified safety function today. The trusted lane runs bounded, testable logic on a pre-certified safety MCU; the smart lane reduces false positives and adds intelligence outside the certified path. This is how the module is both intelligent and certifiable — extending the accepted safety-scanner pattern to a faster sensor, one physics generation later.

The demo · bench-top proof of concept

Sense a fast event. Call it unsafe. Physically stop the machine.

Intentionally humble in scope, designed to prove the core architectural insight: perceive with event vision, decide on embedded compute, and assert real authority over motion through a separate trusted channel — every episode logged, then instrumented for photon-to-stop latency.

  1. Motion sourceA fan, servo sweep, or hobby arm acts as "the robot" — repeatable motion that can be interrupted visibly and electrically.
  2. Event sensingThe GenX320 streams events to the Pi 5 over MIPI CSI-2 the microsecond brightness changes — no frames, no blur.
  3. Smart lane decidesThe Pi clusters events and runs a lightweight rule or model to flag intrusion or unsafe motion, then asserts a stop line.
  4. Trusted lane enforcesThe STM32 monitors that line and deterministically drives the cutoff — separate from the AI compute.
  5. Power interruptionA relay cuts the actuator path; the mechanism visibly stops. Physical authority, not an alert.
  6. Black-box loggingEvent stream, timestamps, detector output, stop-assertion timing, and power-state transitions are recorded for replay and latency measurement.

Why event-based

Sensing that reports only change.

A dynamic vision pixel fires an asynchronous event the instant its brightness changes — no frames, no global shutter, no exposure. That single property is the physical basis for ultra-low latency, milliwatt standby, and blur-free high-speed reaction.

  • µs timingEvents timestamped at microseconds — the temporal resolution a reflex needs.
  • ~120 dBEach pixel self-adapts; no saturation under harsh, variable lighting.
  • No blurFast objects stay sharp — frame motion blur simply doesn't exist.
  • Data ∝ motionA static scene emits near-zero output — the physics behind milliwatt always-on.
  • CommodityNow purchasable as a Raspberry Pi 5 starter kit — research hardware became a purchase order.
